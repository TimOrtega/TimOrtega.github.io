VEX High Stakes

A competition robot is only as capable as the systems working together inside it.

Team Captain2024–2025 High StakesC++ / CAD / Pneumatics
The story

As VEX team captain, I led a team of five engineers in bringing mechanical structures, electrical components, and software together into a competition-ready robot. High Stakes pushed me to think about the complete system—from how a ring enters the mechanism to how the robot acts autonomously.

Project focus

01 / The game

High Stakes.
Many moving pieces.

The 2024–2025 VEX High Stakes game challenged teams to score rings on stakes, place mobile goals, and climb at the end of a match. Matches combined an autonomous period with driver-controlled play, so the design had to serve both programmed actions and a human operator.

For our robot, I designed and built a mechanism that reliably stacked six rings on a mobile goal stake. That required coordinating the mechanical design with motor and sensor placement and the control code driving the mechanism.

Game context: REC Foundation High Stakes game overview.

02 / Systems integration

A mechanism is
only one part of a robot.

My role connected hardware, software, electronics, high-pressure pneumatic hardware, and sensors. The mechanical structure had to accommodate actuators and wiring; the electronics and sensors had to support the control system; and the software had to make the assembled hardware behave as intended.

I programmed and tuned autonomous routines in C++ to score rings and position the robot during the autonomous period. Integrating those routines with the physical mechanism meant paying attention to sequencing, repeatability, and the relationship between a command and what the robot actually did.

As captain, I led five engineers through that integration work. Our team placed 24th at the State Championship during the 2024–2025 High Stakes season.

Engineering in practice

Build the interfaces.
Make the whole system work.

Robotics taught me to look beyond an individual mechanism. Mechanical design, sensing, actuation, and code all meet at interfaces—and getting those interfaces right is what turns a collection of parts into a working robot.
